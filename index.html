Introduction

A long time ago in the West, there were two brothers. Having been orphaned when they were still children (the younger only six years old), the two were inseparable. The older was named Sawyer. He was a small, broad-shouldered fellow with rough hands and a furrowed brow. He was quiet, with gray eyes always active, always surveying his periphery. Nothing took place in their little town without Sawyer taking notice.

The younger brother's name was Chaplin, but most everyone called him Chip (except for Sawyer, that is, who always said, "What else do we have of our mother if not for our names?"). Chip was tall and thin and always with a song. "He has the gift," their mother used to say.

After the death of their parents, Sawyer, twelve years old at the time, packed up everything of value in their home: six pounds of rice, their mother's silver locket, their father's shotgun, and a copy of Ralph Waldo Emerson's Nature and loaded them up in a cart pulled by their lazy-eyed mule, Cynthia. The two boys headed west. Cynthia, that tough old beast, drew her last just after crossing into California. They buried her right there in the desert, and Chip sang a hymn of his own composition over the grave.

It was there in California that the two boys settled down and sought their fortune. Ten years later, the gold rush came. Here we will start our story. Having panned a considerable amount of placer gold from a mountain creek, the brothers decided to find the source. They purchased a crate of black powder and blew a chunk out of the cliff from which the creek sprang forth. While clearing out the rubble, a fateful strike of Sawyer's pickaxe revealed something surprising: a small dark opening in the rock face. Moving rocks away by hand, the brothers saw that the small aperture was, in fact, a door in the form of a perfectly regular archway, carved clean from the stone with uncanny precision.
